# FUNIX — Customer Psychology Context

**Release and evidence:** Consult `FUNIX_REGISTROS_VIVOS.md` for the approved release, dated observations and evidence. This document expresses customer-research hypotheses and interpretation rules; it does not certify a universal customer profile or a measured market position.

## 1. Purpose and boundaries

This file owns **customer behavior and research hypotheses**: why customers seek repairs, what they may value, how they perceive quality and price, and how FUNIX should investigate decisions and objections. It is not a sales script (`04_sales.md`), campaign guide (`08_brand_assets.md`), economic model (`02_business_model.md`) or approved market study. The interpretation and precedence rules are in `00_master_index.md`.

## 2. What we know, and what remains a hypothesis

FUNIX leadership has reported **strong price sensitivity** in quotation conversations, sometimes accompanied by negative reactions to price, while observing that customers often still expect a good repair. This is a **reported experience**, not a statistically validated percentage of the market, a precise acceptable premium, or proof that any individual prospect buys on price alone.

Do not treat illustrative differences discussed by management (10%, 15%, 30%, 50%) as verified acceptance thresholds. Obtain matched quote evidence and consistent loss-reason records before drawing market conclusions. The exact relative importance of price, trust, quality, time, convenience and guarantee remains context-dependent and unmeasured.

**Foundational distinction:** Price sensitivity is not rejection of quality. A customer who requests several quotes or negotiates may be a strong fit. Refusal of safe, honest, properly scoped work in favor of knowingly defective or deceptive shortcuts is a different question of fit (`01`, `04`, `05`, `06`).

## 3. The customer's multiple kinds of value

Customers purchase an actual **repair**, not only a feeling of trust. Their decision may combine:

- **Functional value:** technical suitability, finish, alignment, durability and operation of the repaired item.
- **Economic value:** acceptable total price, payment terms and the cost of future problems or repeated visits.
- **Practical value:** timing, mobility, ease of contact, realistic dates and convenient approvals.
- **Relational value:** respectful explanations, honesty about uncertainty and dependable response.
- **Emotional value:** confidence, relief and reduced fear of being misled or disappointed.

None is universally dominant. Trust can reduce perceived risk and make alternatives easier to assess, but **does not automatically eliminate price sensitivity**. Conversely, a lower quote need not overcome perceived safety, quality or reliability risks. These are hypotheses to investigate, not behavioral laws.

## 4. Situations and sources of uncertainty

The experience differs among a recent collision, damage discovered after parking, insurance-controlled work, a long-standing scrape and a planned aesthetic service. Customers may arrive worried, hurried, embarrassed, calm, technically informed or focused exclusively on logistics; do not presume the same emotional state for every person.

Potential questions include:

- What must actually be repaired, and what is optional?
- What is included or excluded, and what could change after inspection?
- Why do two estimates for the same visible damage differ?
- Is repair versus replacement technically appropriate?
- Will paint, fitment and function meet the agreed standard?
- When is the vehicle realistically available, and who will communicate delays?
- What happens after delivery if an issue appears?

Repair is often difficult for a layperson to evaluate in advance, especially hidden work. **Credible explanations, authentic evidence, precise scope and legitimate warranty information may help establish confidence and improve comparisons.** This is a testable mechanism; do not present it as a measured FUNIX conversion result.

## 5. Situational segments — working lenses, not fixed personas

| Situation | Likely decision questions | Communication implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality-conscious, price-sensitive repair customer | Is this good work at a defensible price? | Clear quote and comparable scope; no contempt for bargaining. |
| Urgent mobility or collision customer | Can I use my vehicle again safely and within a credible timeframe? | Triage urgency, dependencies and realistic next step. |
| Technically uncertain customer | How serious is the damage and what repair is required? | Explain alternatives and limits without inventing certainty. |
| Detail-sensitive customer | What will the finish look like and how is it checked? | Specify the accepted finish and inspection criteria; avoid vague perfection claims. |
| Insurance-/partner-influenced customer | Who approves, pays and sets the timetable? | Explain controllable and externally dependent steps. |

These categories may overlap. They do not prescribe a service package, price or production workflow and should be revised if real customer data does not support them.

## 6. Decision process and hypotheses

Use a flexible journey, not a mandatory psychological funnel:

1. **Discovery:** customer identifies damage or a desired improvement and finds possible providers.
2. **Assessment:** customer evaluates service fit, technical credibility, accessibility and likely economics.
3. **Contact:** responsiveness and clarity provide early evidence of competence.
4. **Quotation:** an intelligible, correctly scoped quote permits meaningful comparison.
5. **Decision:** price, trust, timeline, alternatives and budget may lead to approval, postponement or rejection.
6. **Execution:** updates and reliable handling of exceptions shape confidence.
7. **Delivery and aftercare:** actual quality, commitments and response to problems inform satisfaction, recommendation and return.

A small well-defined job may move quickly; a technically uncertain or high-stakes case may need inspection and explanation. `04_sales.md` owns the steps and authorizations. Do not create friction solely to appear consultative.

## 7. Price comparisons and objections: interpretation discipline

A customer saying “expensive” is **evidence of a stated objection**, not proof of its underlying cause. Plausible explanations include genuine price disadvantage, a different scope, insufficient evidence of quality, budget constraint, payment terms, urgency, prior bad experiences or comparison with a non-equivalent offer. Several causes may coexist.

Capture, when the customer volunteers or reasonably provides it:

1. **Stated objection:** the customer's own words, without psychologizing.
2. **Comparison quality:** competitor amount and scope when available; matching of services, parts, paint process, guarantee, timeframe, payment conditions and exclusions.
3. **Known outcome:** approved, lost, deferred, still comparing, unreachable or unknown; do not invent the chosen provider or actual reason.

Only conclude “FUNIX is more expensive” for a **documented like-for-like comparison**, with limits noted. A market-wide pricing conclusion needs a suitable sample. A rival's lower number alone is not proof of a technically equivalent service.

The data-capture and commercial action belong to `04`; economic interpretation to `02`; metrics to `09`; marketing language to `08`; data governance to `07`.

## 8. Evidence worth collecting first

Favor a small, sustainable learning loop rather than an elaborate research program. For a defined period and repair family, record total quotes issued, known approvals, pending cases, declared objections and any truly comparable competitor quotations. Where practical, classify urgency, referral/source, relevant work scope and lost reason **without forcing false certainty**.

Questions to test:

- Is conversion currently limited by price, response speed, scope clarity, demand quality, capacity or another factor?
- Which repair families show the clearest difference between our prices and equivalent local quotations?
- Do real proof of workmanship, clearer scope and reliable dates improve approvals or decrease price conflict?
- Does a simpler, faster quote process outperform longer consultation for routine work?
- Do reported priorities differ by repair situation and payment channel?

Measure over time and review segments only if sample size and evidence permit. Avoid overinterpreting a few especially emotional conversations. Current learning priorities and responsible staff must be specified in `FUNIX_REGISTROS_VIVOS.md`; `10_strategy.md` governs prioritization.

## 9. Communication implications, not scripts

Speak directly about the actual repair, reasonable alternatives, conditions, timing and price. Communicate technical competence through correct explanations and verifiable examples rather than superiority slogans. Avoid assuming every person needs emotional reassurance, aggressively defending price, or describing competitive value as a luxury service.

The internal *“amigo engenheiro”* reference means helpful expertise without condescension; it is not a claim that an actual engineer reviews each quotation. Marketing examples must comply with `06` and the execution guidance in `08`.

## 10. Safeguards for interpretation

- Do not universalize a psychological ranking or treat the CEO's observations as representative statistics.
- Do not exclude price-conscious customers by default.
- Do not assert FUNIX is the least expensive or technically superior absent comparable evidence.
- Do not equate an objection to price with missing trust, or dismiss a real price disadvantage as a communication defect.
- Do not turn research hypotheses into automated scoring that denies appropriate customers access or rights.
- Do not confuse a favorable customer impression with reliable technical results or sustainable economics.

**Working proposition:** Earn trust through technically responsible execution and clear human dealings, while learning which combinations of value, price and convenience actually cause FUNIX customers to choose, postpone or decline a repair.
