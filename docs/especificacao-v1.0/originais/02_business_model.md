# FUNIX — Business Model and Economic Logic

## 1. Purpose and boundaries

This file is the **single conceptual owner** of FUNIX's revenue model, service and channel economics, cost and contribution logic, economic pricing guardrails, working capital, and investment economics. It describes **how the business can create and capture sustainable economic value**, not how a technician performs a repair or how a salesperson negotiates an estimate.

Use `00_master_index.md` for precedence and information status; `10_strategy.md` for the enterprise thesis and sequencing; `09_decision_framework.md` for trade-offs, authority and metric definitions; `05_operations.md` for repair families, technical standards, flow and effort calibration; `04_sales.md` for pricing policy, discount delegation, quote comparisons and commercial execution; and `06_legal_and_policy.md` for applicable customer and legal protections.

**Status:** this is a conceptual proposal. No present contribution margin, price advantage, cost baseline, customer mix, capacity or pricing floor is asserted without dated evidence in `FUNIX_REGISTROS_VIVOS.md`.

## 2. Where the business earns money

FUNIX provides body repair and automotive paint, with a working focus on small and medium damage. Revenue can come from direct retail customers, insurers and associations, other institutional or partnership arrangements, and complementary aesthetic services. These are **service lines and channels**, not assumptions that one has intrinsically better economics.

| Line or channel | Working economic role | What must be established |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct collision/body-and-paint** | Core repair offer, with potential to influence scope presentation, customer acquisition and prices | Qualified demand, comparable-market price, customer-acquisition cost, actual contribution and collection |
| **Insurer / association / institutional** | Possible recurring demand and capacity utilization, often with third-party terms | Who controls rates, scope and approvals; payment timing; administrative effort; actual contribution; concentration exposure |
| **Aesthetic services** | Complementary offer and possible productive diversification | Family-specific demand, skill/equipment competition, rework, acquisition cost and contribution |
| **Other partnerships / future offers** | Conditional opportunities, not established growth commitments | Incremental profit and cash, operational fit, compliance, dependence and effects on existing work |

The decision to favor a line or channel depends on **incremental contribution, scarce capacity, quality, demand reliability and cash**, not ticket size or a blanket preference for retail over institutional work. The customer-research hypotheses behind segmentation belong to `03_customer_psychology.md`; channel priorities belong to `10_strategy.md`.

## 3. The economic mechanism — efficiency must be captured

Operational improvement can reduce wasted materials, rework, unnecessary handling, waiting, paid overtime, avoidable outsourcing and other costs. It may also release bottleneck time to deliver **more accepted comparable work with the same relevant resources**. This is an intended route to advantage, **not proof that the advantage already exists**.

An efficiency gain contributes economically only when at least one mechanism is demonstrated:

1. **Additional profitable demand is served** with recovered productive capacity and without sacrificing delivery quality.
2. **Avoidable costs are reduced**, including defects, overtime, external services, materials or future expense that is genuinely not incurred.
3. **The sales or service mix improves** within actual demand, technical competence and capacity constraints.
4. **Customer value and reliability improve** enough to strengthen conversion, retention, collection or defensible pricing.
5. **Risk or necessary capacity is preserved** at an economically justified cost, such as competent backup coverage of a critical role.

**Important counterexample:** with fixed payroll, unchanged demand and no avoided expense, fewer touch hours per job may create spare time without raising short-term cash profit. The released capacity still has potential value, but it is not realized profit. If the restraint is demand rather than production, improvements to quotation conversion, acquisition, offer and price comparability may deserve priority before adding output capacity; `10` determines sequencing from evidence.

Do not presume a gain until changes in job mix, paid hours, outsourced labor, quality returns, collection and downstream bottlenecks are considered. An apparent unit-cost improvement caused only by allocating fixed overhead over an assumed higher volume is not a realized saving.

## 4. Cost architecture and unit economics

Maintain **two complementary views**, with explicit treatment of taxes and accounting period:

- **Incremental / contribution view** for accepting work, managing constrained resources and evaluating channels: attributable net job revenue less genuinely variable or incremental costs of acquiring, producing, delivering and collecting the job.
- **Full operating-result view** for survival and reinvestment: aggregate contribution less fixed and period operating costs, financing and other applicable charges, with treatment consistent with accounting records. Cash availability is assessed separately from recognized profit.

Do not use a cost allocation as if it were a cash saving. A cost may be fixed over one planning period and adjustable over another (for example, staffing, rent or contracted capacity). Record the chosen period and the behavior of each cost.

### 4.1. Minimum job economics breakdown

| Component | Economic treatment to validate |
| --- | --- |
| Gross approved price and actual price paid | Record approved scope, later authorized changes, discounts, refunds and billing/collection status separately. |
| Taxes and payment charges | Apply the applicable tax treatment and actual transaction/financing costs; never assume one rate for all channels. |
| Parts and outsourced work | Identify actual purchase/service cost, freight, returns, defects, procurement effort and timing where material. |
| Paint, consumables, materials and energy | Capture attributable usage or a **documented, tested** estimate by repair family; reconcile to aggregate purchasing. |
| Direct labor | Record real technical effort by family/stage; classify payroll cost consistently as short-run committed capacity versus incremental labor/overtime/outsourcing. |
| Acquisition and administrative work | Include truly incremental media, commissions, transaction and job-specific handling; measure fixed commercial overhead separately. |
| Rework, warranty and non-quality | Record related effort/material and expected exposure without silently hiding corrections inside normal production. Avoid double counting. |
| Fixed overhead and capacity | Track rent, baseline payroll, depreciation, administration, maintenance and facility costs at period level; allocate only for defined analytical purposes. |

**Working definitions:** job contribution = net job revenue minus appropriately defined job-attributable incremental/variable costs; operating result = aggregate contribution less committed/fixed operating costs and other charges for the period. Exact formulas, inclusion rules and metric names must be reconciled with the common metric dictionary in `09`, Section 6, and the actual accounting method. **Neither gross ticket nor contribution alone is net profit.**

### 4.2. Repair families and effort standards

Use the operational **repair families defined and validated by `05`** to compare equivalent work. For recurring families, develop a cost profile that connects actual parts, materials, labor effort, rework, delay and relevant scarce resources to collected revenue and contribution.

Do not treat the developing quotation system's **energy factors** as labor hours, touch time, resource consumption or unit cost. Their calibration is an open dependency (`GOV-06`): compare representative accepted repairs with actual effort and costs, document variation, validate on later samples, and only then use a factor in margin or productivity claims. Any decision to expand automated or voice-driven quoting on these assumptions must be reviewed jointly by `02`, `04`, `05` and `07`.

A simple family-level estimate with known uncertainty is preferable to precise-looking figures derived from unvalidated factors. Keep technical process standards in `05`, metric denominators in `09`, and system schemas in `07`.

## 5. Prices, contribution protection and economic value

**Commercial ambition:** offer customers reliable, technically appropriate work at an economically compelling price, supported where possible by proven operating efficiency—not luxury signaling, perpetual discounts or underfunded work.

Pricing has three distinct layers:

1. **Economic guardrails (`02`):** use realistic costs, risk, committed capacity, tax and payment terms to inform a sustainable contribution range or floor by family/channel where sufficient data exists. Differentiate routine floor logic from exceptional, approved short-run capacity decisions. **No numeric floor is established by this file.**
2. **Commercial policy (`04`):** establish quote construction, comparison of equivalent scopes, valid discount bands, exceptional pricing procedure and sales authority. Staff must not infer discount permission from an economic model.
3. **Approval and binding limits (`09`, `06`, `00`):** respect delegated authority, contractual scope, consumer rights and technical quality regardless of the price being offered.

A price reduction is not automatically earned by a process improvement. Leadership must decide whether validated gains should improve customer price, contribution, resilience, team capability or investment capacity in light of actual demand, competitors, cash and risk.

**Competitive** is not a universal assertion that FUNIX is the cheapest. Validate price position through documented comparisons of equivalent repair scope, parts and materials, workmanship/finish, warranty, delivery assumptions, taxes and payment terms. A customer saying “too expensive” is useful evidence of an objection, **not proof of a quantified market premium**. The protocol for gathering and interpreting quote comparisons belongs to `04`; customer-behavior hypotheses belong to `03`; public comparison claims require `06` review.

## 6. Demand, conversion and channel economics

For each major service family and channel, distinguish **available qualified demand**, **quotes issued**, **quotes approved**, **approved work waiting to start**, **work actually delivered**, and **collected cash**. The stages are different economic constraints and must not be collapsed into “more leads” or “more cars”. Operational queue and effort measures belong to `05`; definitions belong to `09`.

Working diagnostic situations:

| Observed condition (to verify) | Economic question before investing |
| --- | --- |
| Underused resources and too little approved work | Can acquisition, response, comparable pricing, trust evidence, follow-up or offer fit increase **profitable approvals**, or can costs be avoided? |
| Approved work queue at a constrained stage | Which flow, equipment, readiness, training or staffing change improves **accepted output** and contribution at that stage? |
| Work waiting for parts/insurer authorization | Can dependency management or contractual terms shorten idle capital and lead time without compromising consent or quality? |
| Work sold but material/cash cannot be funded | Is **working capital** rather than sales or physical throughput the immediate constraint? |
| Many low-price jobs with complex handling | Does realized contribution after processing, rework, payment delay and constrained capacity justify the channel? |

For institutional arrangements, separate scenarios in which the payer **sets prices, labor rates or payment timing** from direct retail quotes. Efficiency may primarily protect contribution rather than reduce the customer price where FUNIX lacks pricing control. Record counterparty terms and concentration risk; do not presume every insurer or association operates identically.

## 7. Cash, working capital and financial resilience

A profitable approved quote does not automatically fund its execution. Model the timing and uncertainty of **deposits, parts payments, labor/overhead, card settlement, insurer receivables, supplier terms, taxes, warranty costs and collections**.

Maintain a practical short-horizon cash forecast with starting available cash, expected receipts by probability/date, committed disbursements, near-term operating needs and material uncertainties. Distinguish **recognized revenue, billed amounts, receivables and collected cash**. Do not fund expansion from nominal sales or optimistic collections.

Evaluate how each channel affects cash tied up, disputed receivables, payment terms and dependence on any one payer. Commercial payment plans belong to `04`; legal collection/contract controls belong to `06`. Financial reporting source, currency and period belong in the dated live register or approved accounting records, not as fixed numbers here.

## 8. Investment and capacity economics

Before major spending on **equipment, automation, personnel, space or new channels**, evaluate at the whole-system level:

- precise operating or customer constraint addressed; a purchase that does not relieve a relevant constraint may have little return;
- baseline versus realistic alternatives, including better scheduling, process change, maintenance, training, outside support or continued current method;
- full implementation and operating cost: acquisition, installation, financing, labor, training, maintenance, consumables, downtime and decommissioning where relevant;
- expected incremental accepted output, avoided cash costs, quality and delivery effects, demand and mix requirements;
- working capital, cash downside, reversibility, payback or other appropriate return measure, and management attention required;
- ramp-up risk, utilization uncertainty and technical/legal approvals.

A staffing increase may be justified by **coverage and continuity**, even without an immediate increase in average labor productivity. Conversely, automation or a larger facility may be unattractive despite technical feasibility if demand, cost, reliability or cash do not support it.

Any future expansion, including the larger-facility ambition, is governed by `10` and `09`. This file provides the economic test—not a footprint target, timing commitment or investment approval.

## 10. Boundary rules for recommendations

- **Do not equate** ticket, gross revenue, contribution, cash, utilization, throughput and profit.
- **Do not claim** a market price or cost advantage that has not been validated on comparable work.
- **Do not use** energy or time proxies as proven costs before calibration; record uncertainty rather than fabricate margins.
- **Do not prioritize** a channel, service, discount, machine or payroll expansion without examining the present constraint, actual incremental economics, customer/technical requirements and cash.
- **Do not presume** that higher productivity yields profit without demand, cost avoidance or other capture.
- **Do not relax** repair quality, safety, worker protections, informed approval or technical integrity for economic targets.
- **Do not restate** global strategy, detailed repair procedures, marketing scripts, or the metric dictionary here; follow their owning documents.

**Economic test:** *For comparable conforming work, does the action improve customer value and sustainable enterprise contribution or cash after its full incremental costs and risks, given real demand and constrained resources? If we do not know, what small measurement or reversible trial would make the decision better?*
